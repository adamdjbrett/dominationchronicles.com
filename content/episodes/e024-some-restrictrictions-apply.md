## Introduction

In this episode, Peter d’Errico and Steve Newcomb compare the final Universal Declaration of Human Rights with its first draft. The June 1947 draft stated plainly that everyone has the right to resist oppression and tyranny. The December 1948 version does not. Steve and Peter discuss what this change means for the ideas of “human,” “rights,” and “the state.” They also ask why the document only hints at the domination system.

## Show notes

Steve Newcomb explains how he found the issue. At the 2023 Parliament of the World’s Religions in Chicago, he attended a panel on the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and noticed the third paragraph of the preamble: “Whereas it is essential, if man is not to be compelled to have recourse, as a last resort, to rebellion against tyranny and oppression, that human rights should be protected by the rule of law.” To Steve, tyranny and oppression are synonyms for domination. When he raised the point, the panelists dismissed it. Later he found the Drafting Committee’s outline of June 4, 1947. It has no preamble, and its Article 29 is one sentence: “Everyone has the right, either individually or with others, to resist oppression and tyranny.”

Peter reads the final Article 29, which has three parts. First, everyone has “duties to the community in which alone the free and full development of his personality is possible.” Second, everyone “shall be subject only to such limitations as are determined by law solely for the purpose of securing due recognition and respect for the rights and freedoms of others and of meeting the just requirements of morality, public order and the general welfare in a democratic society.” Third, “these rights and freedoms may in no case be exercised contrary to the purposes and principles of the United Nations.” In their reading, the right to resist became a duty to the community and a way to file complaints. Peter notes the irony that the two drafting languages, English and French, come from nations founded on revolutions that claimed a right to resist. Steve reads the articles on slavery, torture, arbitrary arrest, and fair tribunals as places where the document acknowledges domination without naming it.

The talk then turns to what “rights” and “the state” are. Peter quotes Justice Holmes, who described a right as a prophecy that a court will rule in your favor. He also cites Calvin’s Case (1608), where the English judges held that allegiance is a quality of the mind and soul that “cannot be circumscribed within the predicament of ubi.” It is a personal bond to the king, not a bond to a place. Steve traces liege, allegiance, obligation, and ligament to a root meaning “to bind,” and points out that the oath does the binding. Both describe the state as an idea that people enact through their behavior, backed at every point by the possibility of lethal force. Peter’s son Adrian puts it as “perception plus participation creates reality.” The episode title comes from Peter’s t-shirt: “Land of the free. Some restrictions apply. Void where prohibited.”

Steve then rethinks the word “dehumanization.” In the Christian European mental world, his ancestors were classified as barbarians, and there had to be a process for making them human. That process meant losing a free way of life, subjection, baptism, a Christian name, and forced labor in the California missions. In Steve’s account, dehumanization is the projection of “heathen,” “pagan,” and “infidel.” Humanization is the sequence of steps that forces people under domination. Read this way, the Declaration insists on humanization as the domination system defines it. Peter closes with Article 19 on freedom of expression and argues that the Article 29 limitations cancel it. Steve closes by saying that original nations still have a right to a free and independent existence, and that a claim of a right of domination is only a claim.
