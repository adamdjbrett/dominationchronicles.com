Back from our summer break, we resume the podcast by discussing the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. We compare the June 4, 1947 draft outline of the document with the final version adopted on December 10, 1948. Two texts, a little over a year apart. In the first, a plain right to resist tyranny. In the second, a duty to obey and a “last resort.”

Steve came to this the way he comes to most of these findings: by reading the document in the room. At the 2023 Parliament of the World’s Religions in Chicago, he attended a panel on the Universal Declaration. Before it began, he looked up the text and his eye fell on the third paragraph of the preamble: “Whereas it is essential, if man is not to be compelled to have recourse, as a last resort, to rebellion against tyranny and oppression, that human rights should be protected by the rule of law.” Tyranny and oppression are two synonyms for domination. When Steve raised the point, the panelists were dismissive. It had no significance for them.

Some months ago he went looking for an earlier draft. The Drafting Committee’s outline of June 4, 1947 has no preamble at all. Its Article 29 is one sentence: “Everyone has the right, either individually or with others, to resist oppression and tyranny.”

That sentence evidently provoked discussion, because the final Article 29 says nothing of the kind. Peter reads it in the episode. It has three parts. First, everyone has “duties to the community in which alone the free and full development of his personality is possible.” Second, in exercising his rights and freedoms, everyone “shall be subject only to such limitations as are determined by law solely for the purpose of securing due recognition and respect for the rights and freedoms of others and of meeting the just requirements of morality, public order and the general welfare in a democratic society.” Third, “these rights and freedoms may in no case be exercised contrary to the purposes and principles of the United Nations.”

All of that language took the place of one straightforward sentence. Somewhere in the drafting an assumption crept in: if there is something called human rights, then there is no need to resist tyranny and oppression, except as a “last resort.” You go and claim your right before a tribunal of some kind. The right to resist became a duty to the community, a duty to obey, and a procedure for filing complaints. Peter notes the irony that the two drafting languages, English and French, belong to nations founded on revolutions that claimed exactly that right.

Steve reads the articles on slavery, torture, arbitrary arrest, and fair tribunals as places where the Declaration acknowledges domination without ever naming it. The document only hints at the domination system. It never says the word.

From there we slow down and ask what a “right” is, and what “the state” is. Peter quotes Justice Holmes, who described a right as a prophecy that a court will rule in your favor, and Calvin’s Case (1608), where the English judges held that allegiance is a quality of the mind and soul that “cannot be circumscribed within the predicament of ubi.” Allegiance is a personal bond to the king, not a bond to a place. Steve traces liege, allegiance, obligation, and ligament to a root meaning “to bind,” and points out that it is the oath that does the binding. The state, rights, human, person: these are ideas. We talk about them as if they were physical reality, but they are part of a mental world, enacted by persons who say they are the agency, the court, the president, up and down the chain of command, and backed at every point by the possibility of lethal force. Peter’s son Adrian put it well: perception plus participation creates reality.

Steve also rethinks a word we have both used for years, “dehumanization.” In the Christian European mental world, our ancestors were classified as barbarians, heathens, pagans, infidels, and there had to be a process for making them human. In California that process meant the loss of a free way of life, subjection, baptism, a Christian name, and forced labor building the mission structures. Massive numbers did not survive it. The dehumanization, Steve now suggests, is the initial projection of “heathen” and “infidel” onto our ancestors. The humanization is the sequence of steps that forces a people under domination. Read this way, the Declaration insists on humanization as the domination system defines it. Those of us who are the descendants of our so-called barbarian ancestors are now counted on the human side of the equation because we are living under domination as their descendants.

The episode title comes from Peter’s t-shirt: “Land of the free. Some restrictions apply. Void where prohibited.”

Peter closes with Article 19 on freedom of expression and argues that the limitations in Article 29 cancel it. Steve closes with the view-from-the-shore: our original nations still have a right to a free and independent existence, and a claim of a right of domination is only a claim.
